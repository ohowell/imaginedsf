The Eddy map is one of the most important in the history of San Francisco. The survey established patterns that would guide the future growth of the city, while also documenting features of its past that would soon be erased by boomtown urbanization.

In 1849 the newly established municipality of San Francisco hired a US Navy officer named William Marston Eddy to survey the city. This was the first survey of San Francisco in the American period. Eddy’s was also the first survey to show the dramatic impact of the Gold Rush. Most estimates put San Francisco’s population at 200 in 1846 and 1,000 in 1848. This was a dramatic increase, but it paled in comparison to the boom that followed the discovery of gold in the Sierra Nevada. Between 1848 and 1849, the population increased from 1,000 to 25,000. By 1852 the figure was 34,776. Buckelew’s and O’Farrell’s 1847 surveys were scaled to respond to the 500% increase in population but were inadequate to a 2,400% increase. This explains why a new survey was needed so soon, and why it covered so much larger an area. Eddy continued to expand and refine his initial survey, and the 1852 version of his map is presented here.

The city’s primary impetus for conducting the survey was to bring order to a chaotic scramble for land along the waterfront and to the west of the established city. This scramble began in earnest shortly after the onset of the Mexican-American War in 1846 when the *USS Portsmouth* claimed San Francisco for the United States without firing a shot. Thereafter followed a period of nearly two years, until the signing of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848, when San Francisco was under American control, but was not yet American. This circumstance created a legal limbo: was the city governed by Mexican or American laws? Squatters and real estate speculators took advantage of the ambiguity, pressuring the *ayuntamiento* (city council) to certify land sales and create legal titles that might not otherwise have been approved. In response, the *ayuntamiento* petitioned the “military-appointed Governor of California, General Stephen Kearney, for the right to sell” not only lands to the west of Yerba Buena, but also the “beachfront and tidelands” (Ferguson). As the historian [Laura Ferguson](https://doi.org/10.1177/0096144218759030) has noted, Mexican law reserved beach and tidelands for public use, while American law clearly designated such lands as property of the federal government (see also Dow, 84). Yet Kearney gave the city approval to divide and sell so-called water lots in spite of the fact that this action almost certainly violated any and all laws that might have governed the circumstance. This outcome likely would have been possible only in the interstices between national regimes, but not under either of them.

Legally or otherwise, the city now had the authority to sell land within its boundaries. In 1849, the municipality had hired Eddy as a contractor to determine where those boundaries would be, and how the land within them would be subdivided (Booker, 50; Hittell, 150). In 1851 the municipality hired him on a permanent basis as the City Engineer, and he continued to expand and refine his map. The Eddy survey was a cadastral map, establishing street and property lines, laying out 1,400 new lots (Ryan, 272). An artifact of the colonial period persisted in these surveys: the Spanish Crown had mandated that new territories be surveyed in *varas*–a unit of measurement equal to about 33 inches. Richardson employed the *vara* in his original survey of Yerba Buena. The Vioget survey standardized the lot size at 50 x 50 *varas*, and Buckelew and O’Farrell continued this practice. The Eddy map shows how the most densely populated area of the city, around the eastern shoreline, had already been subdivided into lots of 50 x 16 *varas*, but with the new lots west of Montgomery Street Eddy employed the original unit of 50 x 50 *varas*.

There are at least half-a-dozen versions of the Eddy map in the archives. All versions follow the Buckelew map precisely, except in switching the street names of Dupont and Stockton. (Dupont was renamed Grant Street, after President Ulysses S. Grant, following the earthquake of 1906.) “Imagined San Francisco” uses this version because it is the most complete. Unlike earlier versions, the 1852 survey shows how some downtown streets continued into the bay as wharves, including Broadway, Pacific and Market Streets. This is the first version to show the South of Market curve into the Mission grid to the south–indeed, this is the first map by anyone to show this peculiar feature of San Francisco’s street pattern. The 1852 map is also one of the only versions that shows the land claims to the west of Larkin Street. The 1858 Land Claims map shows how chaotic and conflicting these claims really were. Eddy’s map brought order to this tangle of competing claims at least on the east side of the city.

While the 1852 Eddy map is the most complete, one earlier version merits discussion because it contains a feature that does not appear as such on the present map, and because the map played an important role in determining subsequent patterns of development. [Eddy’s “Red Line” map](https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/58921?ln=en&v=uv#?xywh=-4427%2C0%2C14472%2C7493) marked the boundaries of the city, past and future. Not to be confused with the twentieth-century maps that assessed mortgage lending risk (often in a discriminatory manner) Eddy’s map featured two red lines: the inner line described the pre-contact coastline, while the outer line described the planned future extent of the city.

Some of this land had already been filled, while some was platted but was still underwater at high tide. All the area within the outer red line was to be under the control of the city, while any waters or future lands outside the line would be under the jurisdiction of the State of California (Dow, 84). A comparison with modern maps shows that all the new lots that Eddy here described eventually did become land. The only exception is two city blocks that appeared in the modern Aquatic Cove, at the northern termini of Polk and Larkin Streets. While the red lines themselves do not appear on the 1852 version, in fact all the information is still contained in the present map. The inner red line corresponds closely to the original coastline as described on this map with a dark line, while the outer red line corresponds perfectly to the blocky, outer dark line that here marks the boundary between land and bay.

The Eddy maps are also notable in that they are the first to show the so-called Lagoon Survey. This small, disconnected survey appears at the northwest edge of the newly planned grid, but is tilted about 30 degrees westward. The survey sits at the edge of the Washerwoman’s Lagoon, so named because several laundries were established there during the 1840s. Surprisingly, there is no peer reviewed scholarship on the Lagoon Survey, but in his guidebook Rand Richards states that Eddy himself conducted the survey in 1847. The Buckelew and O’Farrell surveys had already been completed, so why would the Lagoon Survey not be oriented to the expanding grid? Richards and other commentators have offered a variety of possibilities. The grid might have been oriented to the shore of the lagoon, the original coastline, or even the planned Market Street. Whatever the case, the new grid would eventually subsume the smaller development. The Lagoon Survey appears in the Coast Surveys of 1853, 1859, and 1869 as well as the 1858 Land Claims map and the 1864 Butler map. The 1873 Langley Directory map is the first to document the erasure of the Lagoon Survey; it does not appear on any map after 1869. In the twenty-first century, there remain only a few small traces of the Lagoon Survey. Two dead-end alleys–Blackstone Court and Grenard Terrace–are oriented to the Lagoon grid. These alleys, and the properties immediately surrounding them, are the only surviving remnants of the Lagoon Survey.
