Henry G. Langley, “The San Francisco Directory for the Year 1873,” S.D. Valentine & Sons Publisher, 1873.

Source: [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/sanfranciscodire1873lang/page/n1/mode/2up)

Before google, and before the advent of the telephone book, the principal source of local information in San Francisco was the [Langley Directory](https://archive.org/details/sanfranciscodire1873lang/page/n113/mode/2up). This 1873 fold-out map comes from the fourteenth edition. Like the directories in other major American cities in the mid-nineteenth century, the Langley Directory was much more than a precursor to the phone book. The directory is best understood as a combination of the yellow pages, booster pamphlet, and municipal report. The directory contained not only the addresses of residents and businesses, but also this detailed map. It also contained essays extolling the vitality of the local economy, and a host of data that would be reported in the municipality’s Annual Report by the end of the century. Thus, Langley included descriptions of the structure of local government, as well as aggregate real estate assessments, property tax revenue, infrastructure expenditures, demographics, statistics on bonded debt, and so forth.

The map itself is notable in several respects. This is the first map to show Columbus Avenue–here labeled as Montgomery Avenue, the Street Naming Commission changed it to Columbus Avenue in 1909. This is also the first map to show Golden Gate Park. Political boundaries are also represented on the map: the large numbers superimposed over downtown, South of Market, and the Western Addition indicate wards. Labels on many of the wharves show which operations they were dedicated to. See, for example, the “PMSS Wharf. Steamers for China and Panama” at First and Townsend Streets. Principal rail lines are clearly represented. The main cemeteries are clearly shown, including the “Jewish Cemetery” that would later become Dolores Park. The map is also one of the first to present an imagined San Francisco: the extent of bay fill shows the unrealized aspirations of the local establishment. A comparison to any modern basemap shows that Hunter’s Point, in particular, here extends much further into the bay than it ever would in reality.
