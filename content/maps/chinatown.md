In 1885, the San Francisco Board of Supervisors appointed three of its members to form a special committee to investigate living conditions in Chinatown. This map was included in [the committee’s 100-page report](https://archive.org/details/chineseinsanfran00farw/page/n7/mode/2up).

Postbellum San Francisco was at the center of anti-Chinese agitation in the United States. The most notorious expression of this movement was the Workingmen’s Party of California, a political party founded by a labor organizer and Irish immigrant named Dennis Kearny in the 1870s. With a self-proclaimed “anti-coolie” platform, the Party argued that Chinese immigrants presented unfair competition to white labor, and advocated not only for a total halt to immigration, but also for the forceable removal of the Chinese who were already present in the United States. But Kearney and his followers were only the most radical of what-was-in-fact a broad based movement that included not only more moderate elements of union labor, like the city’s Building Trades Council, but also intellectuals and land reformers like Henry George, also a resident of San Francisco (see Venit Shelton, especially Chapter 3). The 1885 Board of Supervisors’ report on Chinatown–and the associated map presented here–stand as evidence that the municipal government of San Francisco was also a powerful member of the anti-Chinese movement.

The stated impetus for the report was to thoroughly document “the continued and persistent violation of the sanitary regulations in that portion of the city occupied by the Chinese” (Board of Supervisors, 160). While conditions in Chinatown were indeed crowded and often unsanitary, the report nevertheless presented a profoundly racist and sensationalist portrait of the neighborhood. Historians and planners have long understood that so-called slum conditions are best explained by observing the collision between poverty, on the one hand, and the scarcity of urban land, on the other. In the nineteenth-century American city, people with limited resources had little choice but to crowd into small living quarters. Limited resources also made upkeep of property a challenge–a circumstance that was exacerbated by unreliable urban services, like garbage collection. But rather than focusing on how to resolve these systemic issues, real estate interests and local governments often scapegoated poor people, particularly poor people of color, by pointing to their supposed proclivities and preferences–the innate inferiority–of so-called slum dwellers. This same reasoning was prevalent in elite New Yorkers’ observations of the tenements in the Lower East Side at the end of the nineteenth century.

The 1885 report on Chinatown is a textbook example of a municipality laying the blame for inhumane living conditions at the feet of the people who endured those conditions, and it did so in terms that were often virulently racist. In order to illustrate the tenor of the discourse, it is useful to quote at length. The report found that in Chinatown

it may truly be said that human beings exist under conditions (as regards their mode of life and the air they breathe) scarcely one degree above those under which the rats of our waterfront and other vermin live, breathe and have their being. And this order of things seems inseparable from the very nature of the race, probably must be accepted as borne with–must be endured, if it cannot be cured–restricted and looked after, so far as possible, with unceasing vigilance, so that, whatever of benefit, “of degree,” even, that may be derived from such modification of the evil of the presence among us, may at least be attained, not daring to hope that there can be any radical remedy for the great, overshadowing evil which Chinese immigration has inflicted upon this people (166).

In this wholly representative passage, “people” were the white residents of San Francisco, while Chinese were “the presence,” an “overshadowing evil” that was “scarcely one degree above” rats and vermin. The conditions in which Chinese lived were here explained primarily by their “race proclivities” (209). A proper reading of this report must situate it not only in San Francisco’s anti-Chinese movement, but also in the context of late nineteenth-century scientific racism, which relied upon a misreading of Darwin’s evolutionary biology to assert that non-white races were in fact an under-evolved subspecies of humanity. Chinese were human, but only “scarcely.”

This passage highlights another persistent theme: for the committee, the root of the problem was immigration. The report continually gestured towards the need for stringent immigration laws, but argued that until such laws could be passed, only the enactment and vigilant enforcement of new municipal laws could mitigate the conditions of Chinatown. The committee did “disclaim any sympathy” with more radical proposals, like those advocated by Kearney, to use “physical force in any form to drive . . . \[the Chinese\] from among us, because . . . \[the committee\] do not recognize this method as in any sense a practical solution of the question as to how it is best to deal with a fully recognized public evil” (164-165). The committee hoped that its careful study would promote “more intelligent discussion and more intelligent action than has been had before, and will go far, it is also hoped, to correct public sentiment in the East upon this all-important question” (165).

Reference to “the East” showed up often in discussions not only of race, but also of political radicalism in San Francisco. “The East” primarily meant New York. Twenty-first century American urbanites typically perceive the existence of intercity cultural identity contests, whether they choose to engage them or not. San Francisco’s rivalry with Los Angeles dates to the early twentieth century, but the rivalry with New York is rooted in the city’s founding. But while the nineteenth-century preoccupation with New York might be partly explained as an intercity cultural contest, much more was at stake. The early development of San Francisco relied heavily on financing from New York banks, insurance companies, and real estate investors. San Francisco elites complained of the superior attitudes of New Yorkers, but also expressed anxiety that the “East” perceived San Francisco as an instant city, a foreign and barbarous place. This impression was not helped by the presence of a large Chinese population, but nor was it helped by the presence of Irish immigrants forming political parties, advocating labor radicalism, and baying for violence against another immigrant group. How might New York’s perceptions have adjusted had the municipal government sided with the latter immigrant group? So while the committee might have displayed a modicum of moderation on the question of the physical removal of Chinatown, that restraint must be understood in the context of a public relations campaign. Cultural capital might have been at stake, but so was economic capital.

Removal was off the table, so until the federal government passed more stringent immigration restrictions, the committee viewed municipal law as a bulwark. Accordingly, the report sketched a series of proposed ordinances. Surprisingly, this included fines for white property owners. In the only passage that advanced a systemic critique of the economics of land, the report did identify white owners of Chinatown property as “*particeps criminis* \[accomplices\] in this great wrong against civilization” (207). Prefiguring Jacob Riis’s critique of landlords in *How the Other Lives* (1890) by five years, the report noted that white property owners “had no special reason to regret the occupation of his premises by Chinese insofar as the rate of revenue derived therefrom is concerned, for that revenue, if your Committee are credibly informed, has been materially larger than could have been obtained from any other class of tenants” (207). Riis developed this critique in his exposé of the [African American tenements in New York](https://books.google.com/books?id=zhcv_oA5dwgC&pg=PA148&source=gbs_toc_r&cad=2#v=onepage&q&f=false), showing how landlords leveraged racial prejudice to create artificial scarcities of housing for minority residents. Though Riis traded in racist tropes himself–and presented New York’s Chinatown in terms only slightly more charitable than the committee did San Francisco’s–he also showed how the conditions of the tenements were essentially the result of capitalist exploitation of poor people of color. The San Francisco Board of Supervisors did not see the circumstance so clearly, presenting landlords only as accomplices rather than the principal antagonists, as Riis did. But the committee did advocate that the “property-owner in Chinatown must be made to feel his responsibility in this matter before Chinatown can ever be brought to a level with common public decency” (207). The property owner would be made to feel this responsibility through fines for overcrowding or other violations of municipal codes, and the report contemplated putting liens on properties that were out of compliance (212).

As for the other ordinances that the report sketched, the map itself provided a guide. The main uses that the report mapped are the same that it proposed to regulate. Prostitution was to be tightly regulated, as were Chinese laundries; “gambling dens” and “opium resorts” were to be closed. In addition to these regulations, the report contemplated limiting the number of occupants permitted per block, using the most densely populated block outside of Chinatown–meaning the most densely populated block inhabited by whites–as the ceiling (212). Though the report opposed physical removal, the committee did express the hope that the strict enforcement of a suite of new municipal ordinances would mean that “San Francisco will soon cease to be a paradise or even an attractive place of habitation for the Mongolian” (212).

The map is color-coded: beige indicates “General Chinese Occupancy”; pink indicates “Chinese Gambling Houses”; green is “Chinese Prostitution”; yellow indicates “Chinese Opium Resorts”; red indicates “Chinese Joss Houses” (or Chinese Temples); and blue indicates “White Prostitution.” The key also notes that the map only covers the first floor of the buildings. Since most of the buildings had multiple stories, the uses of the upper floors are not covered here. Based on a reading of historic fire insurance maps, it is likely that the upper floors were primarily housing. Notations on individual buildings give more detail as to use. MDSE indicates Merchandise. Depending on the amount of space available, buildings might be labeled Ch. or C., both of which are short for Chinese. Thus “C. Gen MDSE” indicates Chinese General Merchandise. The map highlights multiple instances of mixed use, such as “C. Fruit Stand Lodging,” and “C. Undertaker and Cigar Factory.” “W.” indicates White occupancy, as in “W. Shooting Gallery” and “W. Light House Saloon.”
