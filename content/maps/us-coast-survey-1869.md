US Coast Survey, “San Francisco Peninsula,” US Coast Survey, 1869.

Source: [David Rumsey Map Collection](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/s/w38301).

The US Coast Survey’s 1869 map tracks the remarkable growth of San Francisco since the 1859 survey, but also shows that the pace of the development of the downtown shoreline slowed. In fact, there appears to have been some consolidation of the wharf system just south of Market Street. The 1857 and 1859 surveys show close to ten smaller wharves that do not appear in the 1869 survey. However, the 1869 survey also shows two new larger wharves, one just south of the Market Street Wharf, and another extending into the bay from Howard Street. The map also shows the earliest enclosure of Mission Bay, with a rail line on trestles. The 1864 Butler map labels this line the “North & South Beach” Railroad. The line extended from Fourth Street, south to the point that is labeled “Pt. of the Rocks” here but is labeled “Pt. San Quentin” in the other Coast Surveys. Point San Quentin itself here featured four or five new wharves that connected to the new developments around Potrero Hill and the Mission District. The rail line then extended south from Point San Quentin to the base of Hunter’s Point.

While the shoreline in the southern half of the city was steadily developed, the expansion of the city into the interior of the peninsula was dramatic. The 1859 survey shows that was little built in the southern half of the city since 1853, excepting the cluster of housing around the Mission Dolores. But by 1869 the Mission complex had densified considerably, and development now extended as far south as Bernal Hill. A new rail line also wended its way through the nascent Mission District. The 1857 and 1859 surveys show that development did not extend far west of Mason Street, but by 1869, the built environment extended to Divisadero Street. The 1869 survey also shows the first road to transect the San Francisco Peninsula: the Point Lobos Toll Road–later renamed Geary Boulevard–now extended west to the Cliff House, overlooking the Pacific Ocean.

One of the most notable features of the 1869 map is that it provides a way to track San Francisco’s role in the Civil War. In spite of its geographic isolation from the principal theaters of the war, San Francisco was key to the Union cause. Shipments of gold from the Bay financed the Union Army and were a clear target not only for Confederate mercenaries, but also potentially for the British Navy, with whom military planners feared that the Confederacy might form an alliance. While most Californians supported the Union, there was also a strong presence of Confederate sympathizers in the state (National Park Service). All of these considerations led the US to fortify the shipping channels out of the Golden Gate. In 1861 the Army stationed five officers and 100 men at the Presidio; by 1865 there were 50 officers and 1,360 men (84-85). While the 1859 Bay Survey showed only four buildings at the Presidio, the 1869 Survey showed more than 50. In addition to various small outbuildings, the new structures included barracks, officers’ quarters, hospital buildings, a chapel, a “stone magazine” for munitions storage, stables, and “laundresses quarters” (82). A smaller military buildup can also be observed on Hunter’s Point, Fort Point, and Black Point, labeled “Pt San Jose” in the 1859 survey, and known since 1882 as Fort Mason. A buildup is also visible on Alcatraz Island, which first became a prison–originally for military personnel–in 1861.
