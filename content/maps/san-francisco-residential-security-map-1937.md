Home Owners’ Loan Corporation (HOLC), “Residential Security Map for San Francisco,” HOLC, 1937.

Source: National Archives II, RG195, HOLC, location 450, 68:6:2/ box 147. Also available at [Mapping Inequality](https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/data/CA-SanFrancisco#cityData).

The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation (HOLC) was created in 1933, as a part of FDR’s New Deal, for the purposes of helping to stabilize the housing market and assisting homeowners in danger of foreclosure by purchasing delinquent loans from lenders. Between 1933 and 1936, the corporation purchased 1,017,821 such loans, which amounted to 20% of all owner-occupied, nonfarm homes in the country. Between 1936 and 1940, the HOLC conducted a survey of housing in all U.S. cities with populations of at least 40,000. (239 cities met that criterion.) The resulting Residential Security Maps described mortgage lending risk, which was determined by weighing a number of attributes against one another. These included land use; age and condition of structures; accessibility of transportation, retail, and recreation facilities; ethnic, racial, and socioeconomic characteristics of the population; and lenders’ perceptions of the areas, among other considerations. The areas that HOLC deemed the safest bets for mortgage lending were given an “A” rating and were shaded green on the residential security maps; the next-best areas, the “B” areas, were shaded blue; the “C” areas, where cautious lending was advised, were shaded yellow; and those areas that were considered high risks for mortgage lenders were rated “D” and were shaded red. The HOLC’s maps are often regarded as the original redlining maps–and indeed some private lenders subsequently used the maps for this purpose–but it is important to note several things. The HOLC has already made all of its loans before it conducted the Residential Security study, meaning that the maps did not guide lending decisions for the corporation. Moreover, the HOLC gave more loans in areas that it would later shade yellow and red on its maps, than it did to the better-rated areas. Finally, while race was a factor in determining lending risk in San Francisco, the most important factor was mixed land use. This accounts for the fact that only two of the seventeen red-shaded areas hosted minority communities, and wealthy areas like Nob Hill were deemed hazardous lending risks and were shaded red on the maps.
