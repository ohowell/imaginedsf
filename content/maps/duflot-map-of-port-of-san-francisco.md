In 1839 the French government sent an explorer named Eugène Duflot de Mofras to survey the Pacific Coast of North America. [Duflot’s account](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=mdp.39015027936783&seq=15) focuses on the contests between European nations to claim control of California, and suggests that the French should consider the possibility of establishing themselves there (Hittell, 98). As with Beechey’s account \[link to it\] Duflot documented the precipitous decline of the Presidios and of the Mission system, but did so with a much more sympathetic tone towards the Catholic Spanish. “For foreign writers to slander the Spanish clergy from whom they have received the most generous hospitality is indeed deplorable. With typical English coldness, Captain Beechey and Mr. Forbes, men imbued with the intolerance of Protestantism, are inclined to ridicule and cast insinuations on the activities of the Fathers” (Duflot, 141).

Duflot arrived in San Francisco in late 1841. He described a sleepy village: “Founded only a few years ago, it has at the outside not more than twenty houses, which belong exclusively to foreigners and are used merely as places to store goods coming in by ship” (Duflot, 227). The description is striking considering that only eight years later gold would be discovered in the Sierra Nevada, and [the population of San Francisco would increase 2400 percent in a single year](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199329175.013.1053).

Though no one could have predicted such a frenzy, Duflot did understand San Francisco’s great potential as a center of commerce, an observation that has particular salience in light of his description of the Presidio:

The fort has been so completely abandoned that a ship could easily send its small boats over the to the shores below and without attracting the attention of the presidio carry off the cannons that could be easily rolled down the cliff. This point, furthermore, is too high to permit accurate firing on ships that pass below, for vessels come within range only at the neck of the bay, and batteries to be effective would have to be erected at a lower level (Duflot, 229).

Seven years later, it would be the Americans, rather than the French, who would take advantage of these inadequate defenses.

But Duflot’s sociological observations and military assessments took up only a fraction of his account of the San Francisco Bay. He focused on describing the bay itself, and on offering navigational tips. A comparison with Beechey’s 1833 survey shows that Duflot borrowed heavily from the Englishman’s map. Duflot showed five structures in the village of Yerba Buena that do not appear on Beechey’s survey. But the largest difference is in the northeast corner of the map where Duflot corrected Beechey’s error of showing the Carquinez strait as being fed by three rivers (Harlow, 373).
