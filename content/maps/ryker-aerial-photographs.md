Harrison Ryker was an early innovator in aerial photography who patented a number of surveying instruments and techniques. In addition to his work for the U.S. Forest Service, he is known for his survey of San Francisco. In 1938, the Works Progress Administration (a New Deal agency) hired Ryker to photograph the city from the air. The result was [164 photographs](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/view/search/who/Harrison+Ryker?q=ryker+san+francisco&sort=Pub_List_No_InitialSort%2CPub_Date%2CPub_List_No%2CSeries_No) which are here composited into a [single image](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~217219~5504219:Composite--1-164-San-Francisco-Aeri?sort=Pub_List_No_InitialSort%2CPub_Date%2CPub_List_No%2CSeries_No&qvq=q:ryker%20san%20francisco;sort:Pub_List_No_InitialSort%2CPub_Date%2CPub_List_No%2CSeries_No;lc:RUMSEY~8~1&mi=19&trs=680). The Ryker photographs provide the most detailed record of the built environment exactly as it was on the eve of World War II. A comparison to the modern Aerial Imagery (also under Basemaps) is instructive. Areas of particular interest are downtown and the eastern waterfront. From 1938 to the twenty first century, Candlestick Point extended further into the bay, rail yards gave way to stadia and a research campus, while the office landscape densified and extended South of Market. The comparison also highlights dramatic changes in the western neighborhoods. While the Sunset District was largely built-out in 1938, the western reaches of the Richmond District were still dominated by sand dunes.
