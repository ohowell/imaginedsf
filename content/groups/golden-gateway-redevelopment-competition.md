From the early twentieth century through the 1950s the blocks just north of Market Street and west of the docks contained a series of low-rise buildings that served as the city’s primary produce market. By all accounts this was a bustling district, but it was also inefficient and congested. As San Francisco emerged from the Second World War the city’s leaders envisioned a new knowledge economy, and the produce market occupied land that had obvious value for office space as well as for high-rise housing. Responding the increasingly influential Finance, Insurance, and Real Estate (FIRE) lobby, the San Francisco Redevelopment Agency (SFRA) targeted the area for urban renewal.

![From San Francisco News-Call Bulletin.](/assets/images/aac-0526.jpg)

**1945 photo showing congested streets in the produce market area of downtown San Francisco. Source: San Francisco Public Library, Historic Photograph Collection, Image ID: AAC-0526.**

At the close of the 1950s, the SFRA announced a design competition. They received eight serious applications, each from a major property developer teaming with an architecture firm. The submissions were evaluated by a committee consisting mostly of high-profile architects, and one prominent mortgage banker. The panel was chaired by Mario Ciampi, a local architect best known for the Berkeley Art Museum, and who did extensive consulting work with the SFRA and the San Francisco Department of City Planning. Other prominent members included Minoru Yamasaki, the designer of the ill-fated twin towers of Manhattan’s World Trade Center, and Louis Kahn, perhaps best known for the designs of the Exeter Academy Library and the Salk Institute. The panel’s role was advisory, so their final [report](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=uc1.b000983409&seq=3&view=1up) did not rank submissions, or select a winner. But the report leaves little doubt that they favored the scheme they discussed last, the proposal of developers Perini and Associates, partnering with the architects Wurster, Bernardi & Emmons with DeMars & Reay. This was the proposal that the SFRA ultimately selected, an outcome that was typical of design competitions. Construction began in 1962 and was completed in 1986. [The project created approximately 1,400 units of housing, 3.5 million square feet of office space, and about 12 acres of public space](https://sfocii.org/completed-project-areas/golden-gateway). The final project includes the Alcoa Building, designed by Skidmore, Owings and Merril, and the Embarcadero Center, designed by John Portman.
